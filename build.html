    Here is what each line on the chart shows, and what we use it for.

    Jump to finding more parameters on a Skew-T

    A parcel is a small, imaginary portion of air that we can follow as it rises or sinks. Using a parcel this way is called parcel theory.

    The math behind it can get complicated. A Skew-T lets us work through much of it on the chart: its background lines already show how a parcel's temperature changes as it rises, and how much water vapor saturated air can hold. By following them, we can compare the parcel temperature with the environmental temperature and identify features such as the LCL, LFC, EL, CAPE and CIN.

    Parcel theory is a simplified picture. For example, real rising air also mixes with the air around it.

    The background lines

    These are printed on the chart before any weather is added. They stay the same from day to day.

    Pressure lines (isobars)

    Flat lines of equal pressure, in millibars (mb). 1000 mb is near the bottom and 100 mb is at the top. Their spacing follows the logarithm of pressure, so distance up the chart roughly matches height. That is the "log-P" in the name.

    What we use it for: saying how high something is. Every measurement is plotted at its pressure.

    Temperature lines (isotherms)

    Lines of equal temperature in °C. On an ordinary graph they would point straight up. Here they tilt to the right: the "skew" in Skew-T. Air usually gets much colder with height, and tilting the lines keeps those measurements on the chart and makes them easier to compare.

    What we use it for: reading temperature and dewpoint. Follow a slanted line down to the numbers along the bottom. Those numbers stop at -40 °C, so for colder values, count slanted lines to the left of the -40 line, 10 °C apart.

    Dry adiabats

    They show how the temperature of unsaturated air changes as it rises or sinks without trading heat with its surroundings: about 10 °C per kilometer. Rising air cools, and sinking air warms along the same lines.

    What we use it for: following a dry adiabat to track a lifted parcel before it becomes saturated, from the ground up to the LCL.

    Moist adiabats

    They show how saturated air cools as it keeps rising. Condensing water releases heat, so it cools more slowly than dry air, especially low down where the air is warm.

    What we use it for: following a moist adiabat to track a lifted parcel after it becomes saturated, from the LCL upward, inside a cloud.

    Mixing ratio lines

    Each line marks one saturation mixing ratio: the grams of water vapor in each kilogram of dry air when the air is saturated.

    What we use it for: finding moisture. Read the mixing ratio line at the dewpoint to see how much vapor the air has, or at the temperature to see how much it could hold. To help find the LCL, follow a mixing ratio line up from the ground dewpoint until it crosses the dry adiabat from the ground temperature.

    The weather plotted on top

    These come from measurements, so they change with the weather. Radiosondes, small instrument packages carried up by weather balloons, send their readings to the ground by radio. Real soundings have many more readings than the made-up examples on this site.

    Environmental temperature

    The temperature of the surrounding atmosphere at each height.

    What we use it for: seeing how the environmental temperature changes with height. A layer where it gets warmer going up is called an inversion. The parcel temperature is compared with it.

    Dewpoint

    The dewpoint is how cold the air would have to be, at the same pressure, to become saturated. It is never higher than the environmental temperature.

    What we use it for: judging how moist the air is. Where the dewpoint is close to the environmental temperature, the air is humid. Where they are far apart, it is dry.

    Wind barbs

    They sit to the right of the chart. The long staff points to where the wind comes from. Add up the marks to get the speed. For example, two full barbs and one half barb make 25 knots. Speeds are rounded to the nearest 5 knots. A knot is one nautical mile per hour, about 1.15 miles per hour.

      What we use it for: seeing how wind speed and direction change with height. That change, called wind shear, affects how storms are organized.

      Lifting a parcel of air

      Imagine pushing a parcel of ground-level air upward and comparing its temperature with the environmental temperature.

      Parcel path

      The parcel temperature is the temperature of the lifted parcel at each height. It follows a dry adiabat until it becomes saturated, then a moist adiabat.

      What we use it for: comparing the parcel temperature with the environmental temperature, to see where the parcel would be warmer or colder than its environment.

      LCL (lifting condensation level)

      Where the parcel becomes saturated.

      What we use it for: estimating the cloud base for air lifted from the ground.

      LFC (level of free convection)

      Where the parcel temperature becomes warmer than the environmental temperature. The parcel is then less dense and can keep rising without being pushed. Not every sounding has an LFC, and one sounding can have more than one.

      What we use it for: finding the height the parcel must reach before it can rise on its own.

      EL (equilibrium level)

      Where the parcel temperature stops being warmer than the environmental temperature.

      What we use it for: finding the top of the layer where the parcel temperature was warmer than the environmental temperature.

      CIN (convective inhibition)

      The shaded area where the parcel temperature is colder than the environmental temperature. CIN is an amount of energy, written as a negative number of joules per kilogram (J/kg). The farther from zero, the bigger the barrier. A layer that holds air back is often called a cap.

      What we use it for: judging how hard it is to get air to its LFC. According to p. 346 of Wallace and Hobbs (2006), when the size of CIN is above 100 J/kg, deep thunderstorms are unlikely without outside help such as daytime heating or a strong front.

      CAPE (convective available potential energy)

      The shaded area where the parcel temperature is warmer than the environmental temperature. It is also measured in J/kg. On the Learn chart it fills the space between the parcel temperature and the environmental temperature, from the LFC to the EL.

      What we use it for: judging how much energy rising air could have. More CAPE allows stronger updrafts. A large value does not mean a storm will form.

      To stay simple, Learn, Practice and Test compare actual temperatures. The Full chart in Explore also allows for moisture (virtual temperature), so its CAPE, CIN, LFC and EL are different.

      Finding more parameters on a Skew-T

      These build on the lines and parcel ideas above. The steps for the parcel path, LCL, LFC, EL, CIN and CAPE are with their descriptions. Each entry has its own zoomed chart showing only the lines you need. Follow the numbers on the chart alongside the written steps. Unless an entry says otherwise, the values are for the made-up spring storm example. They are rounded, and reading a real chart by eye gives estimates.

      The groups start with readings taken at one level or along one line, then move a parcel, and end with indices that combine several readings.

      How to start reading a sounding

      1. Compare the environmental temperature with the dewpoint to see where the air is humid or dry.
      2. Look for layers where the environmental temperature falls slowly or rises with height.
      3. Lift a parcel and find the LCL, then any LFC and EL.
      4. Compare the CIN with the CAPE.
      5. Check how the wind changes with height.

      This is a starting point, not a forecast. Next, explore the chart or start practicing.
