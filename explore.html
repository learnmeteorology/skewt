Start with two lines

  • Temperature of the air
  • Dewpoint: the temperature the air would have to cool to, at the same pressure, to become saturated. Cool it that far and water vapor can start to condense, as dew or cloud droplets. At the same pressure, a higher dewpoint means more water vapor in the air.

Where the lines are close, the air is humid. Where they are far apart, it is dry.

Flat lines are pressure, slanted lines are temperature, and the barbs on the right are wind.

Surface-based parcel

Why no CIN? This lifted parcel never gets meaningfully warmer than the air around it, so there is no LFC and no CAPE. This chart only gives a CIN value when there is an LFC, so it shows no CIN value or blue shading. That does not mean the air can rise easily.

How to read these numbers

  • CAPE adds up all the red, and CIN all the blue, from the surface to the highest EL.
  • There can be more than one red layer, each with its own LFC. Only the highest EL is labeled. Layers of 1 J/kg or less don't get an LFC or EL.
  • CAPE, CIN, LFC and EL use virtual temperature, which allows for moisture. The shading lies between the two dotted lines.
  • Learn, Practice and Test leave out this correction to stay simple.

Heights are above the 1000 mb surface. The examples are made up for teaching, not observed data. Not for forecasting.

Guide to the lines

Open Learn more under any line for its theory and math. Temperatures are in °C and pressures in mb unless a lesson says kelvin.

Displayed equations come from Wallace and Hobbs (2006), and page numbers in the lessons refer to that work: Wallace, J. M., and P. V. Hobbs, 2006: Atmospheric Science: An Introductory Survey. 2nd ed. International Geophysics Series, Vol. 92, Academic Press (Elsevier). ISBN 978-0-12-732951-2. It writes pressure in hPa; 1 hPa equals 1 mb. Lessons say where the site calculates something differently.
